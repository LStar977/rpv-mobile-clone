The problem

Being ignored isn't an accident. It's the default.

Decisions that shape your street, your rent and your commute are made constantly, by people who were elected years ago on questions that weren't these ones. Between elections, the channels you're offered — a consultation nobody reads, a petition anyone can fake, a poll of four hundred strangers — are not a voice. They're a suggestion box.

386,698

signatures on the largest petition in Canadian history — dismissed in a single news cycle.

And when people do organise, it gets waved away.

Not because the cause was wrong. Because nobody could prove the signers were real people — and a number that can't be verified is a number that can be dismissed by anyone who finds it inconvenient.

That's the trap. You're told to participate, then handed tools that guarantee your participation can be discounted.

What Represent is for

Governing yourself, not being governed at.

A country is supposed to be its people. In practice most of us get to express that once, briefly, and then watch. Represent exists to close that gap — to make participation continuous, local, and worth something.

Continuous

Not once every four years

Questions arrive as they actually arise — a rezoning, a transit cut, a federal bill. You answer the ones you're entitled to answer, when they matter, not years later.

Local

Down to your own city

Your region comes from your verified ID, so a question about your city is answered by people who live in it — not by the loudest accounts on the internet.

Yours

Anyone can ask

You can put a question to your city yourself. You don't need a campaign, a budget or a press contact to find out where people actually stand.

Where this goes

One voice is an opinion. Enough of them is a mandate.

A single verified vote doesn't move anything. That's honest, and it's not the point. The point is what a hundred thousand of them look like — a number from real, verified residents of one city, published openly, that anyone can check and nobody can pad.

Ignoring a poll is free. Ignoring a verified count of your own constituents, on the public record, is a decision someone has to defend out loud. That is the whole ambition: not to replace the ballot box, but to make the years between elections cost something.

We're at the beginning of that. Represent is live, it works, and it's small. Every verified person makes the next count harder to wave away.

What asking costs today

Calgary asked its residents one question. It took two and a half years.

In 2018 the city put a single yes-or-no question to Calgarians: should we bid for the 2026 Winter Olympics? It is the clearest picture there is of what direct democracy costs when it has to be built from scratch every time.

29 mo

June 2016, when the exploration committee began, to 19 November 2018, when it concluded.

Then it was taken apart.

That's the part that should bother you. None of it was infrastructure. The apparatus was assembled to ask one question and dismantled once it was answered, so the next question starts from zero and costs the same again.

Which is why cities almost never ask. Not because they don't want to know — because at that price you get one question every decade, and you had better be certain it's the right one.

Represent is the standing version.

The verification, the region-gating, the ledger and the count are already built and already running. Asking the hundredth question costs essentially the same as asking the first, and the answer arrives as people vote rather than two years later.

That is the actual unlock. Not a cheaper plebiscite — a world where the questions that never got asked, because nobody could justify the cost, finally get asked at all.

To be clear about what this is

A municipal plebiscite is a legally recognised vote of the electors, open to every eligible resident whether or not they own a phone. Represent is not that and does not replace it. It is the instrument for the hundreds of questions that will never justify a plebiscite — which is nearly all of them.

Why it counts

The reason this isn't just another petition.

Everything above only works if the number at the end is real. Three things make it so.

01

Every voter is a verified person

A government ID and a liveness check, once, through a certified provider. Represent never stores your documents. Nobody votes twice, because nobody can verify twice.

02

No result until it means something

A ballot stays sealed until ten verified people have voted, so nobody joins a side that's already winning and nobody quotes a landslide that was really three votes.

03

The count is on a public record

Every ballot is written to a public ledger as well as our database, so the count can be checked rather than taken on trust. The ledger is running in pilot phase.
